Why the show cares

The book's opening family — four jobs, $90,000 a year, SNAP for groceries — earns what sounds like good money. Run it through the time machine: $90,000 today buys what roughly $44,000 bought in 1998. Their income didn't fail. Its power was drained.

Takeaway

Never compare raw dollars across decades. "Salaries are higher than ever" and "paychecks buy less than ever" can both be true — and right now, they are.

Hours of work to buy it

At the average production worker's wage:

  • 🎓 A year of public college tuition
    1964: ~97 hours ($243 at $2.50/hr) · 2026: ~369 hours ($11,950 at $32.40/hr) — 3.8× more work
  • ⛽ A gallon of gas, on minimum wage
    1950: ~22 min ($0.27 at $0.75/hr) · 2026: ~34 min ($4.08 at $7.25/hr)
  • 🏠 The median new home
    1950: ~2.2 years of family income · 2026: ~4.9 years (new homes) — Harvard JCHS puts all homes at ~5.1×
Takeaway

The middle-class squeeze isn't a feeling — it's arithmetic. The big-ticket items that build a life (home, education) inflated far faster than wages; only the small stuff stayed cheap.

Interactive · 123 years of budgets

Where the paycheck went 🧺

The Bureau of Labor Statistics has surveyed American household budgets since 1901. Tap an era to see how the typical family's spending shifted — food's share collapsed, housing's share climbed, and a new giant appeared: insurance, pensions and "everything else" needed to stay afloat.

Pick an era

How to read it

In 1901, 42.5¢ of every dollar went to food — feeding a family was the economy. By 2024 food is under 13¢, but housing (33.4%), transportation (17%) and healthcare (7.9%) took its place, and "other" — dominated by personal insurance & pensions — nearly quadrupled since 1950. Cheaper calories, pricier life.

Takeaway

Progress showed up in the grocery cart, not the mortgage. The categories that grew are exactly the ones you can't skip — shelter, transport to work, health, and hedging against disaster. That's what "squeezed" means, in data.

Survey methods differ slightly across eras (1901–1950: BLS Report 991; 1960–73: share of current consumption; 1984→: modern Consumer Expenditure Survey incl. pensions/insurance). Direction and scale of the shifts are robust; treat single-point decimals loosely.
Interactive · The spine of the story

The Great Decoupling — and the policy choices pegged to it 📉

For 25 years after WWII, productivity and worker pay rose in lockstep: the economy grew, your paycheck grew. Around 1973 the lines split — and never rejoined. Scrub the timeline to watch the gap open, and tap the markers to see which laws and decisions were signed at each turn. "That divergence is the spine of this book, and it was not an accident."

The water temperature 🐸

How to read it

If pay had kept tracking productivity after 1973, the typical worker's paycheck would carry roughly 60%+ more purchasing power today. Economists debate each cause's weight — technology, trade, union decline, policy — but the book's receipts show every inflection sits next to a signature: Taft-Hartley 1947, the 1971 gold decision, PATCO 1981, NAFTA 1994, China's WTO entry 2001, the 2008 bailout design.

Takeaway

"It was not inevitable. It was not the market. It was chosen." — and what was chosen can be chosen differently. That's Part 2's question, and the blueprint below.

The policy peg

Every cliff on the real line is Congress doing nothing while inflation worked. The book's sharpest counterfactual: had the minimum wage tracked productivity since 1968, it would be roughly $26/hour — about $54,000/year full-time (EPI-based calculation).

Part 1 · The Jobs Number Nobody Wanted

July 2026: the month the water got visible 🎙️

Part 1 opens on one number: payrolls fell 23,000 in July when forecasters expected +83,000 — and revisions quietly erased another 103,000 jobs from May and June. Labor-force participation hit 61.4%, its lowest since 2021: ~264,000 people didn't become "unemployed," they stopped raising their hand.

Monthly job growth — first reported vs revised

The AI thread

The coders built the AI agents; the agents took their jobs. Stanford payroll-data research: employment for developers aged 22–25 fell ~20% from its late-2022 peak while 30+ workers in the same high-AI-exposure roles grew 6–12%. AI isn't deleting the career — it's deleting the first rung of the ladder.

Part 2 · When the Models Break 🤖

The money is already moving to what replaces the workers: Morgan Stanley models a $5 trillion humanoid-robot market by 2050 (1B+ units; robot cost $200k → $50k). Tesla claims a 1,000,000-robot/year factory — while actually-shipping humanoids today are mostly Chinese (Unitree ~5,500/yr, from $16k). Announcements ≠ shipments; watch who owns the stack.

Takeaway

Part 1: where the jobs went. Part 2: who owns what replaces them. The 88-year data above is why the show insists this time be answered with policy, not vibes.

The escape plan · from the book

If policy turned the heat up, policy can turn it down 🛠️

The book's final chapters lay out a three-tier blueprint — every item already proven somewhere in the world or already used by a past U.S. administration. "The blueprint is not missing. The political will to implement it is what has been missing."

🏙️ Tier 1 — What cities & states can do right now
  • Raise wage floors: 8 states & 52 cities are already above $15 (San Francisco: $18.67). California's $20 fast-food minimum raised earnings ~11% with no widespread job loss (UC Berkeley IRLE).
  • "Just cause" firing laws: NYC's fast-food law cut terminations 22% and arbitrary discipline 31% in year one.
  • Paid family & medical leave (California model — costs under 1% of payroll) and fair-chance hiring.
  • Zoning reform: Minneapolis ended single-family-only zoning in 2018 — construction rose, rent growth moderated.
🏛️ Tier 2 — Federal action that needs no new law
  • Classify gig workers properly (NLRB rulemaking — ~25M workers currently earn 20–30% less than comparable employees).
  • Use the $600B federal procurement lever: prevailing wages, paid leave, and union neutrality for contractors (precedents: 2014, 2021 executive orders).
  • Ban non-competes (FTC 2024 rule: ~30M workers, ~$524/yr each in suppressed wages).
  • Transparency mandates: pay-gap reporting, algorithmic-decision disclosure, a public zip-code-level economic-equity dashboard — "disease surveillance" for economic wellbeing.
🗳️ Tier 3 — The legislative horizon
  • Index the minimum wage to inflation and productivity — ending the freeze-by-neglect cycle visible in the chart above.
  • The PRO Act (federal right to organize), universal paid leave, universal childcare (every $1 in early childhood returns $2–$13 — Heckman).
  • A "New GI Bill" for the AI transition: Singapore-style universal learning accounts (~$2,000/adult/yr); trained workers there saw 23% less displacement and 31% higher wage growth. Denmark spends 2% of GDP on active labor policy; the U.S. spends 0.1%.
  • Share the machine gains: a 32-hour week phased over 5 years (Iceland, 2,500 workers: output maintained; global 61-company trial: 95% kept it), automation taxes into a National Skills Trust, and worker equity in the AI their data trains (Mondragon: 81,000 worker-owners, 6:1 pay cap, ~3% unemployment through the 2008 crisis vs Spain's 25%).
🧪 The evidence people don't expect
  • Cash floors don't stop work: Stockton's $500/month experiment — full-time employment rose 28%→40%; the "people will stop working" prediction has been tested 20+ times since 1601 and missed every time.
  • The scale check: a full pro-labor AI package models at ~$280B/yr (Urban Institute-style modeling) — roughly the cost of the 2017 corporate tax cut — for +18% median income vs baseline by 2035 and 30% less poverty.
  • The receipt of receipts: ~$34 trillion shifted from the bottom 90% to the top 10% since 1989 (Fed data via Saez & Zucman). That's the bill. It was itemized above, one policy at a time.
